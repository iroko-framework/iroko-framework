What you are looking at

The Vocabulary of Stewardship

The Iroko Framework is not a database of secrets. It is a structured description of how sacred knowledge is held — who may know what, under what conditions, authorized by whom, and governed by which communities. Think of it less as an archive and more as a map of stewardship.

The traditions documented here — Lucumí, Vodou, Candomblé, Ifá, Palo Monte, Abakuá, and others extending across the Afro-Atlantic world — have survived the Middle Passage, colonial suppression, deliberate misrepresentation, and institutional neglect. They survived because communities governed their knowledge carefully. This framework is built in that spirit.

The architecture

What This Is

The Iroko Framework is a semantic vocabulary system: a structured set of terms, relationships, and rules that allows sacred knowledge to be described, organized, and accessed in ways that honor community authority. It is published in sixteen modules, each named across seven language families — Yorùbá, Haitian Kreyòl, Bantu, Akan, Ge'ez, Lucumí, and Ejagham — because no single tradition holds the center.

Six access tiers determine what any given community member, researcher, or institution may see — from freely public to never exported under any circumstances. All of the vocabulary is published under a Creative Commons Zero license. Anyone may speak the language. Not everyone may enter the house.

The language of the framework

Why the Names Matter

Ewé ModuleYorùbá — sacred plant, leaf

The living botanical material that holds spiritual potency. Plant knowledge, ritual use, harvest protocols, and the governance of ethnobotanical description.

Nkisi ModuleBantu/Kikongo — sacred medicine, spirit vessel

The consecrated object or being that carries spiritual force. Governs orisa, lwa, mpungo, vodun, egun, and all spiritual entities across Afro-Atlantic traditions.

Travay ModuleHaitian Kreyòl — sacred work

The labor of ceremony. Ritual processes from the weekly gathering to the threshold rite.

Ilé ModuleYorùbá — house

The actual institution — the terreiro, the hounfò, the cabildo — that holds the tradition across generations.

Marca ModuleLucumí — the mark, the sign

Divination systems: Ifá's two hundred fifty-six odù, the Dilogún, the Fá of the Fon people. The mark is the message.

Ékpè ModuleCross River — the leopard society

Initiatory societies: Ékpè, Abakuá, Ogboni, Poro, Sande. Knowledge held in graded tiers under authorized stewardship.

Vèvè ModuleHaitian Kreyòl — sacred diagram

Graphic sign systems: Vodou vèvè, Palo firma, Abakuá Anaforuana, Ejagham Nsibidi.

Ngoma ModuleBantu — drum

Sacred music, rhythm, and consecrated instruments. The rhythm carries the tradition.

Sankofa ModuleAkan/Twi — go back and fetch it

The bird that flies forward while looking back. Revitalization, diaspora return, reconstruction of practice that survived fragmentation.

Qal Module ( ቃል )Ge'ez — word

One of the oldest continuously used sacred languages on earth. African sacred language traditions are foundational, not peripheral.

Audience

Who This Is For

This framework was built for communities, not institutions. If you are a practitioner of any Afro-Atlantic tradition looking for a way to describe or preserve your lineage's knowledge — this was designed with your sovereignty in mind. If you are a researcher or archivist — this offers vocabulary that honors what these traditions actually are. If you are encountering these traditions for the first time — this is an invitation to take them seriously as living, governed, intellectually sophisticated systems.

The vocabulary is open. The house has doors. Some of them are yours to open, and some of them are not. The framework makes that distinction visible, which is itself a form of respect.

Access Philosophy

The Iroko Framework separates vocabulary publication from data publication. Any institution can adopt the vocabulary with its own access policies calibrated to its role:

Universities

May publish more openly; drawing from published ethnographies and public archival holdings.

Temple Archives

May publish less; active sacred practice requires stricter community governance of access.

Museums

May mix levels; public exhibits alongside restricted donor collections and repatriation records.

Researchers

Field data governed by community authorization; publication requires community endorsement review.

Iroko Historical Society Publishes
  • ✓ Full vocabulary (all classes, properties, concept schemes)
  • ✓ Minimal public data (scientific names, vernacular names)
  • ✗ Sacred knowledge detail: kept private, API-governed at irokosociety.org

Key Features

  • Field-Level Access Control: six-tier permissions from public-unrestricted through initiated-elder, applied per property
  • Contested Knowledge: assertion reification lets multiple lineage perspectives coexist without forcing resolution
  • Provenance Tracking: oral transmission chains, fieldwork context, epistemic type transparency throughout
  • Sovereign Agency: sacred agents, refusal events, and stewardship mandates model community authority without metaphysical claims
  • Postcustodial Design: communities retain authority; the archive serves the tradition, not the institution
  • Semantic Interoperability: integrates Darwin Core, SKOS, Dublin Core, PROV-O, FOAF, schema.org, OntoLex-Lemon
  • Standards-Aligned: W3C RDF/OWL/SKOS, Linked Open Data best practices
